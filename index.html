International Business

A company that wants to do business in another country has to choose a form to go in. It can simply sell there. It can sign a contract with a local company. Or it can own a business there.

Prof. Abdel Salam Abu Qahf, who teaches this course, puts every possible form on one drawing of his own: 17 ways, called modes of entry, in 3 groups, called options.

This lesson gives you the whole map. The next lessons walk through it piece by piece.

Why he is proud of this drawing

He developed it himself, so it is an Egyptian model, and it has been taught in universities abroad. Most textbooks list only five to seven modes. His has 17, and it ties them to information, control, money, risk, profit and government incentives, all in one picture.

Why the free-zone project has no number

A free zone is an area inside a country with its own special rules. He calls it "a state within a state". The course deals only with investment inside the country itself, which he calls "inland", so he leaves the free-zone project out.

If a question still asks about it: older revision material for this course counts a company that produces and sells inside a free zone as direct investment.

His printed sheet and the whiteboard

Prof. Abu Qahf's drawing of the 17 modes is more than a list. Its arrows carry one message: the deeper a company goes into a foreign market, the more it learns, the more it controls, the more money it needs, the more it risks, and the more it can earn.

Two more arrows, on top, say that governments push companies with incentives. He calls them "pressures", which is the word to watch.

Think of a shop in a city you do not know

You can send your goods to a trader there. It is cheap and safe, but you learn nothing about the customers and you cannot set the price.

Or you can rent and run your own shop there. It is costly and risky, but you meet every customer, you decide everything, and all the profit is yours.

The drawing says the same about countries. Each arrow is one part of that trade-off.

1. The two arrows on top: government "pressures"

The host country is the country the company enters. The home country, also called the parent country, is the country the company comes from.

"Pressures" here does not mean political pressure. It means the incentives and privileges that governments offer. Every host government offers incentives to attract foreign investors. Home governments encourage their own companies to invest abroad.

2. The arrow on the left: market information

The deeper the company goes in, the more information it collects, in amount and in kind. A trading house tells you very little. An agent tells you about the market, the competitors and the prices.

3. The arrow on the right: control of the market

The deeper the company goes in, the more freedom it has to decide how many units to produce, how many to sell, and at what price.

4. The double arrow underneath: investment, risk and profit

All three rise together as the company moves toward the middle of the drawing, where owning sits. He calls the middle the "hot area".

The link with the Uppsala idea

The gradual path is the same idea as the Uppsala model, one of the theories that explain why and how companies go abroad. It says a company goes deeper into a market as its experience of that market and its information about it grow. Those are its two success factors: prior experience, and enough information and knowledge.

His second printed sheet

Before a company chooses a mode of entry, it has to choose where to go and what to sell there. Prof. Abu Qahf says it must "look at the world and search for opportunities everywhere".

He gives three phases, in this order: screen the markets and sites, decide between standardization and differentiation, then pick one of five product planning options.

Phase 1: screen the markets and the sites

Screening means examining every possible country and removing the weak ones. A market is a place to sell in. A site is a place to produce in. There are four steps.

Step 1. Identify the basic appeal

Why this country and not that one? What attracts the company there?

Step 2. Assess the national business environment

Three sides:

  • Cultural. Is the culture friendly or hostile to foreigners? Countries that were once occupied often feel that "foreign investment means foreign occupation".
  • Economic. Market size, monetary policies, taxation policies, natural resources and raw materials.
  • Political. How stable is the country?

Step 3. Measure the market size and the site potential

His example is a canned food company looking at Egypt. It must know how much the industry sells now, how many companies are in it, how they compete, who would work for it, and where the raw materials come from. It must also check whether the country can carry its marketing: distribution, transport, storage, advertising, media.

Step 4. Select the market or the site

If steps 1 to 3 come out positive, somebody senior (the chief executive, or the manager in charge) makes a field trip to the country, at least to study the competition on the ground.

Phase 2: the same everywhere, or different in each market?

Standardization means selling the same product in the same way everywhere. Differentiation means a different product, or different marketing, for each market. To decide, the company asks five questions:

  1. The type of product. Can my product be standardized at all?
  2. How similar are the markets? If the countries are alike (homogeneous), standardize. If they are very different (heterogeneous), there is no standardization: a product for each market.
  3. The media through which the product will be promoted.
  4. Government restrictions on pricing, on production, on imports and exports, and on employing foreigners.
  5. The services available: banking, insurance, storage, transport.

Phase 3: the five product planning options

Two things can change: the product and the promotion message. That gives five options.

The choice of a mode of entry "is dependent upon a wide variety of factors". Prof. Abu Qahf sorts them into three groups: factors that come from the company itself, from the host country, and from the parent (home) country.

His exam cases describe a company and ask for the suitable mode. The clues in the case are these factors. Learn to read them and the answer follows.

Three groups, each pushing the choice

1. Firm-specific factors: the company itself

  1. Its financial resources.
  2. Its previous experience and information.
  3. The attitude of its top management.
  4. The aptitude of its top management.
  5. Skilled people who can be sent abroad.

How they push. A company short of money cannot enter directly: only the indirect modes are open to it. A company with no experience picks a lighter mode. If top management has a negative attitude toward going abroad, it prefers indirect modes: "you can make licensing, you can use an agent".

Attitude and aptitude, in his words

Attitude is how you feel about something. Aptitude الاستعداد is your readiness to actually do it.

"I want to drink tea, and I like tea, so my attitude toward tea is positive. But I am too lazy to make it: I have no aptitude."

2. Host-country factors

  1. How healthy the business environment is: political, economic, social.
  2. Restrictions on foreign ownership.
  3. A hostile culture or attitude toward foreigners.
  4. Government restrictions on business, pricing and production.
  5. Political stability, and relations with neighbouring countries.
  6. Market size.
  7. Everything that makes a location attractive (the location theory).

How they push. Most companies want to control and manage their own project. But where foreign ownership is strictly prohibited, the company cannot enter directly. Where the market is small, the company uses an agent, a distributor or a licence, because it will not invest a lot of money for a small profit.

3. Parent-country factors

The relationship between the home country and the host country. A hostile relationship can stop companies from entering.

His example

When Ronald Reagan was president of the USA, he asked American companies not to invest in the Soviet Union, so that it would not get the new technology. The companies obeyed.

The textbook's five factors

The textbook gives five "strategic factors in selecting an entry mode".

  1. Cultural environment. An unfamiliar culture makes managers avoid investment and prefer exporting or a contract.
  2. Political and legal environments. Instability makes companies avoid large investments. High tariffs or low import quotas encourage producing inside the country. Some governments ban full foreign ownership and require a joint venture. Weak protection of patents makes a company prefer investment, to keep control of its assets.
  3. Market size. Rising incomes encourage investment. A market likely to stay small suits exporting or a contract.
  4. Production and shipping costs. A low local production cost or a high shipping cost favours producing locally. A low shipping cost favours exporting.
  5. International experience. Most companies start with exporting and go deeper as experience grows, although some skip stages.

Somebody else carries your product

1 Trading house

A commercial company that buys and sells inside its own country. One day it finds an opportunity abroad and exports some of the goods it trades in. The producer of those goods has now become international "without any previous plan".

His example

An Egyptian trading house buys canned food from Juhayna, Faragalla and others, and sells it in Egypt. Then it finds an opportunity in Libya and exports some of the goods there. Faragalla has become an international company without planning it: it was internationalized by what the trading house did.

His story: Basma's scarf

Basma travels to Italy and brings back a wool scarf. Her neighbour loves it and wants one. They look for it in a shop at Carrefour and do not find it. The shopkeeper reads the label on the scarf, finds the Italian producer's name and address, contacts the company and imports a number of scarves.

The Italian company has entered the Egyptian market without ever planning to. A company can become international by chance, through a trading house or even through a souvenir.

2 Agent الوكيل

The link between the producer and the final customer. The agent does not own the product: the producer still owns it. The agent works for a commission.

An inclusive agent works with all the companies in the field. An exclusive agent works with one company only.

His examples

A dairy company notices that trading houses keep exporting its products to Libya. So it announces that it wants a Libyan agent.

Hajj Mahmoud El-Araby started as the exclusive agent of Toshiba in Egypt: he sold no other brand.

3 Distributor الموزع

Also a link between the producer and the customer. But the distributor buys the product and resells it for his own account. So the distributor owns the product. There are inclusive and exclusive distributors too.

Do not mix them up

The agent never owns the goods, and earns a commission.

The distributor buys the goods, owns them, and earns the difference between his buying price and his selling price.

5 E-commerce

Selling through the internet, as on Amazon. Many companies that never thought about going international became international this way: a customer at home orders from a company in the USA, France or Korea.

A licence is a permission. The owner of a product lets a local company produce and sell it in a given market, and takes a fee called a royalty.

Prof. Abu Qahf's exams ask about its two types. The whole difference is one question: who is the reason for the licence? If the reason is the host country, it is licensing by necessity. If the reason is the company itself, it is licensing by choice.

6 The licensing contract عقد الترخيص

Three things are involved:

  • The licensor: the owner.
  • The licensee: the local company.
  • The object: the product under licence.

The licensor gives the licensee permission to produce and market the product in a given market, in exchange for a royalty. The royalty is usually a percentage of the licensee's sales.

What is licensed is intangible property: things you cannot touch, such as patents, formulas, designs, trademarks and brand names.

Licensing is found in industrial projects more than in commercial ones. Nestle and Pfizer work this way.

By necessity, or by choice

His exams ask: "What are the types of licensing contracts, and when is each type used?" The answer is two lists. What separates them is whose reasons they are.

By necessity (forced) التراخيص بالضرورة

The reasons come from the host country

The company would have liked to invest, but the country leaves it no better way.

  1. The host market is small and not profitable in the long run.
  2. Political and economic instability in the host country.
  3. The host country does not allow foreign investors into strategic industries.
  4. The host country restricts foreign ownership, fully or partly.
  5. The host country restricts exporting and importing.

By choice التراخيص بالاختيار

The reasons come from the company itself

The company chooses the licence because of its own situation or wishes.

  1. It lacks the money for direct investment.
  2. Its marketing ability and experience in that market are weak.
  3. It is very strong in research, development and innovation (Sony has 5,000 inventions).
  4. It lacks the people to work in foreign markets.
  5. It has no wish, or no ability, to exploit a new market itself.
  6. It wants to sell licences as an alternative to exporting.
  7. It wants to test the market, as a first stage before direct investment.
  8. It wants to enter new markets with no investment cost and the least risk.
To remember it

Necessity: the country says no (5 reasons). Choice: the company says "I prefer" (8 reasons).

The usual trap in multiple-choice questions

A list of company reasons with the label "by necessity" is wrong. Check whose reason it is before you look at anything else.

7 Franchising الامتياز التجاري

In a franchise, the owner (the franchiser) gives a local company (the franchisee) the right to use what it owns, and helps it over a long period. The most wanted item is the brand name.

McDonald's, Pizza Hut, KFC, Burger King, Starbucks and Costa work this way, and in clothing Lacoste and GUESS. So the McDonald's shops in Egypt are franchise contracts, which is indirect investment.

Franchising differs from licensing in three ways:

  1. More control. The owner keeps strict rules on quality, daily management and promotion.
  2. Mostly services. Restaurants, hotels, car dealerships. Licensing is common in manufacturing.
  3. Ongoing help. Training, advertising, advice on location. A licence is usually a one-time transfer.

Licensing

A permission from the licensor (the owner) to the licensee (the local company) to produce and market a certain product in a certain market, in return for a royalty. It is an indirect mode, common in industrial projects.

Run it, make it, or build it for someone else

8 Management contract عقد الإدارة

The foreign company manages all or part of a project owned by someone else, in return for fees or a share of the profits. It is common in hotels (Hilton, Sheraton) and cinemas.

8 Manufacturing contract عقد التصنيع

The foreign company agrees with a local company that the local company will manufacture a product on its behalf.

Examples

Carrefour has a paper tissue factory make tissues that carry the Carrefour name. Marks and Spencer has an Egyptian factory make 1,000 suits to its specifications.

He counts the two contracts together as one mode, number 8.

9 Turnkey investment project مشروع تسليم المفتاح

A foreign company builds a whole plant for a local owner, "from A to Z", until the production lines produce the first unit. Then the plant is handed over to him. The owner only has to turn the key. "Just like the contractor."

His example

An Egyptian businessman wants to set up a pharmaceutical company, or an oil refinery, but has no experience in that industry. He asks a big foreign company such as Pfizer to come to Egypt and build the whole thing.

A turnkey project has three characteristics:

  1. The local owner pays the foreign company fees for its services.
  2. The local owner pays for the equipment and the machines.
  3. After the trial runs of operation and production, the project is handed over to the local owner.

Produce through others, cooperate, or co-own

10 International subcontracting التعاقد من الباطن

One party, the subcontractor, produces and supplies goods or basic components for the other party, the principal, who sells the final product under his own trademark.

His example: Honda

An Egyptian car dealer orders 1,000 Honda cars from Honda Japan. Honda Japan can assemble only 600. For the remaining 400 it asks its branch in Jordan to produce them to the same specification, and may send that branch all the parts.

If no Honda branch has spare capacity, Honda may ask another company to produce the 400 to the same specification. He named BMW, Mercedes, or Nasr in Egypt.

11 Strategic alliance التحالف الاستراتيجي

Two companies cooperate so that each one uses the other's competitive advantage. Company A has a highly skilled marketing team but nothing new to sell. Company B has invented something new (a patent) but cannot market it. Together, each gets what it lacks. No new company is created.

His classic example: EgyptAir in the Star Alliance

When an EgyptAir aircraft lands in Bangkok, Thai Airways serves it: water, food, everything, plus the security inspection. When a Thai aircraft lands in Cairo, EgyptAir does the same for it.

EgyptAir lands in about 100 foreign countries and could never build its own facilities in all of them. You can even book a Thai Airways ticket at an EgyptAir office.

An alliance is among the fastest and least costly ways to enter a market, and it helps to get over the restrictions placed on direct and indirect entry.

12 Joint venture مشروع مشترك

When the market proves promising, the foreign company sets up an investment project together with a local investor, and they share its ownership. The partners come from two different countries. The partnership is permanent. And it is not limited to money: a partner may contribute management, expertise, a patent or a trademark.

His example is the Toshiba and El-Araby assembly plant in Egypt.

Do not mix them up

In a strategic alliance the two companies only cooperate. No new company is born.

In a joint venture they create a new, separate company, and both own it.

  • Turnkey project: build the plant and hand it over when it produces the first unit (Pfizer). Three characteristics: fees for services; the owner pays for equipment; handed over after the trial runs.
  • International subcontracting: part of an order is produced by another party to the same specification (Honda: 1,000 ordered, 600 in Japan, 400 in Jordan).

It happens through the stock market. The company buys the shares and becomes the owner, with no negotiation.

It happens through negotiation between the seller and the buyer. The owner announces that the business is for sale, and the foreign company negotiates the price, the delivery date and the other terms.

The two modes his exams have named

A past exam asked: "Mention briefly the advantages and disadvantages of licences and of agents, for both the host countries and the foreign companies." So these lists are worth knowing by heart. Licences first, agents in the next step.

Licensing for the foreign company

Advantages

  1. A good source of income. The licensee pays for the equipment and the factory.
  2. It limits the theft of patents and illegal copies.
  3. Among the fastest and easiest ways in, especially for small companies.
  4. Less risky than owning in unstable markets.

Disadvantages

  1. Loss of control over operations, quality and the market. Quality differs from country to country.
  2. Loss of the markets neighbouring the licensed country.
  3. A lower return than direct investment.
  4. A weak licensee with an exclusive licence blocks the whole market.
  5. The licensee may become a future competitor.

Licensing for the host country

Advantages

  1. Countries prefer it for ideological reasons, political and economic.
  2. It gives the foreign party no control in the host market.
  3. The country keeps its political and economic independence.
  4. It avoids technological dependence on the foreign party.
  5. It develops national technology and independent strategic industries.

Disadvantages

  1. The country is deprived of modern, advanced technology.
  2. The high cost of obtaining the licences.
  3. A lower inflow of foreign capital.
  4. Weak local competence may fail to exploit the patents.

The same four lists for agents

Agents for the foreign company

Advantages

  1. A presence in the market at low cost, with no operating expenses. Easy and cheap to appoint.
  2. A good source of information and marketing opportunities.
  3. Helps to overcome marketing problems.
  4. The lowest degree of risk.
  5. Keeps the company present in the foreign market.
  6. Much used by small and medium-sized companies.

Disadvantages

  1. The agent may give attention to some products at the expense of others: the one that pays the highest commission.
  2. If the market later proves profitable, the company may not be able to exploit it through the agent.
  3. The real benefit depends on the agent's financial, marketing and technical abilities.
  4. Disagreement over geographic coverage and marketing expenses.
  5. Many industrial customers prefer to deal with the company itself.
  6. Ending the relationship can be costly and difficult.

Agents for the host country

Advantages

  1. The country avoids many of the negative effects of direct and indirect foreign investment.

Disadvantages

  1. The economy may turn commercial: a trading society more than an industrial one.
  2. More economic and political dependency.
  3. The agent may behave against the national interest.
  4. A limited contribution to jobs and to the balance of payments.

The balance of payments is the record of all the money entering and leaving a country.

The pattern

For the company. Light modes: little money and little risk, but less control, less profit, and a possible future competitor. Ownership: full control and all the profit, at high cost and high risk.

For the host country. Light modes: the country stays independent, but gets little capital, few jobs and little technology. Ownership: capital, jobs and technology, with the danger of monopoly and dependency.

Licences, in short

Company, for: income; limits patent theft; fast and easy entry. Against: loss of control; loss of neighbouring markets; lower return; a future competitor.

Host, for: no foreign control; independence; no technological dependence; national technology grows. Against: no advanced technology; costly licences; less foreign capital; weak local competence.

Agents, in short

Company, for: presence at low cost; information; lowest risk; stays in the market. Against: the agent favours some products; a profitable market may not be exploitable through him; it all depends on his abilities; industrial customers prefer the company itself.

Host, for: avoids the negative effects of foreign investment. Against: a trading economy, not an industrial one; dependency; the agent may act against the national interest; few jobs.

The risks of owning

The wholly owned subsidiary carries the non-commercial risks: nationalization, confiscation, compulsory liquidation.

For the foreign company, which two advantages and which two disadvantages do all the light modes share?

Advantages: little money and little risk. Disadvantages: less control and less profit, often with the danger of creating a future competitor.

For the host country, what do the light modes keep, and what do they fail to bring?

They keep the country independent. They bring little capital, few jobs and little technology. Ownership is the reverse: capital, jobs and technology, with the danger of monopoly and dependency.

Nationalization, confiscation and compulsory liquidation are the non-commercial risks of:

  1. The agent
  2. The licence
  3. The turnkey project
  4. The wholly owned subsidiary

Only a company that owns assets in the country can have them taken. This is the price of full control.

Give three advantages and three disadvantages of licensing for the foreign company.

Advantages: a good source of income; it limits the theft of patents; among the fastest and easiest ways in. Disadvantages: loss of control over operations, quality and the market; loss of neighbouring markets; a lower return than direct investment. Also: the licensee may become a future competitor.

Give three disadvantages of agents for the host country.

The economy may turn into a trading one more than an industrial one. More economic and political dependency. The agent may behave against the national interest. A limited contribution to jobs and to the balance of payments.

Distributors

For the company, advantages: the same as agents, plus: they matter most when the product needs after-sales service; they are the best outlet for durable and capital goods; they own the goods, so they carry the risk of selling them.

For the company, disadvantages: the same as agents, plus: the company loses control over the final price.

For the host country, advantages: the same as agents, plus: the distributor offers jobs, trains labour and creates links with other economic sectors.

For the host country, disadvantages: the same as agents.

Franchising

For the company, advantages:

  1. Low cost and low risk for the owner.
  2. Rapid geographic expansion.
  3. The owner benefits from the local manager's knowledge of the culture.

For the company, disadvantages:

  1. Hard to manage many franchisees in many countries. Quality and promotion may differ.
  2. The franchisee loses flexibility: the contract restricts what it may sell and do.
Management contract

For the company, advantages:

  1. Needs no capital.
  2. Entry with a lower degree of risk. Physical assets are not exposed.
  3. Helps to move later toward a joint or wholly owned project.
  4. Keeps the market.
  5. Makes it easier to enter neighbouring markets.

For the company, disadvantages:

  1. The local owner may interfere in management to protect its money.
  2. A clash of interests between the two parties causes organizational, production and marketing problems.
  3. The managers themselves are exposed to political or social trouble.
  4. The company may be training a future competitor.
Manufacturing contract

For the company, advantages:

  1. Needs very limited capital.
  2. No exposure to political risk.
  3. Lower production and marketing costs.
  4. A way over the obstacles to exporting to that country.
  5. Suits companies whose strength is technical and managerial, not productive.
  6. It combines productivity, the advantages of licensing and some of the advantages of direct investment.

For the company, disadvantages:

  1. Hard to keep tight control over the market, the quality and the marketing policies.
  2. The advantages depend on finding an efficient local manufacturer.
Management and manufacturing contracts, for the host country

Advantages:

  1. Technological progress in production, management and marketing.
  2. Better skills for the local workforce.
  3. Fewer negative political and economic effects.

Disadvantages:

  1. The foreign party's full control of the activities creates a conflict of interests with the national party.
  2. A very limited contribution to jobs and to the inflow of foreign currency.
Turnkey project

For the company, advantages:

  1. The company earns fees.
  2. A lower degree of political and non-commercial risk.
  3. The possibility of expanding the investment later.
  4. Needs no capital. Suits small companies.
  5. Each company does what it is best at.

For the company, disadvantages:

  1. Low profitability.
  2. No control at all over operations, the market and quality.
  3. Any failure caused by the local owner harms the company's reputation.
  4. The project may be awarded for political reasons, not for know-how.
  5. It can create a future competitor.

For the host country, advantages:

  1. Fewer negative political and economic effects.
  2. Accepted by Third World and communist countries.
  3. An advanced level of technology.

For the host country, disadvantages:

  1. Its contribution to technology, jobs and the balance of payments depends on the kind of activity.
  2. Limited inflow of foreign currency and capital.
  3. Problems of dependency.
Strategic alliance

For the company, advantages:

  1. Among the fastest and least costly ways in.
  2. Shares the cost of a big investment.
  3. Uses the partner's specific strength and distribution channels.
  4. Gets over restrictions on direct and indirect entry.

For the company, disadvantages:

  1. It can create a future local or global competitor.
  2. Conflict can break the cooperation.
Joint venture

For the company, advantages:

  1. The form of direct investment most accepted in developing countries.
  2. If it succeeds, it helps to win approval for full ownership later.
  3. The preferred form when the government refuses full ownership, or when the company lacks money, people or technical resources.
  4. Suits small and medium-sized multinationals.
  5. Less risk: each partner risks only its own share. Access to the partner's distribution network. The venture looks more "local".

For the company, disadvantages:

  1. Conflict of interests between the national and the foreign partner, worst when ownership is 50-50.
  2. The national partner may later try to push the foreign partner out.
  3. A national partner with weak technical and financial abilities hurts the project.
  4. It needs relatively large capital.
  5. If the national partner is the government, it may set strict conditions on employment and exporting.
Wholly owned subsidiary

For the company, advantages:

  1. Full freedom in management and control of production, technology, output and prices.
  2. Large expected profits, all kept by the parent.
  3. It avoids the problems of the other forms.
  4. Ease of obtaining facilities.
  5. Easy to coordinate all subsidiaries in one global strategy.

For the company, disadvantages:

  1. The non-commercial risks: nationalization, confiscation, compulsory liquidation.
  2. Expensive: the company finances it alone.
  3. High risk, including the risk that buyers reject the product.

For the host country, advantages:

  1. A larger inflow of foreign capital.
  2. A better balance of payments.
  3. Technology that suits the country's development needs.
  4. Direct and indirect jobs.

For the host country, disadvantages:

  1. The dangers of monopoly and dependency.
  2. Conflict of interests between the multinational and its home country on one side, and the host country on the other.
Merger and acquisition

For the company, advantages:

  1. The fastest means of entering a foreign market.
  2. Uses financing opportunities and shares the cost.
  3. Benefits from the other company's advantages.
  4. A strategic tool for raising competitiveness.

No disadvantages are listed in the course material.

"Discuss, using a drawing, strategic alliances as one of the indirect strategies for entering foreign markets."

I tried it. Open the model answer

Definition. A strategic alliance is an agreement between two parties that lets each party exploit and benefit from the strengths of the other. No new company is created. It is mode 11 in his model, an indirect strategy.

The idea. Company A has a competitive advantage that company B lacks (a highly skilled marketing team). Company B has an advantage that A lacks (a new patent it cannot market). They agree that each uses the other's advantage.

His example. EgyptAir in the Star Alliance: Thai Airways serves the EgyptAir aircraft in Bangkok (water, food, security inspection), and EgyptAir serves the Thai aircraft in Cairo. EgyptAir lands in about 100 countries and cannot build its own facilities in each.

Features. Among the fastest and least costly ways of entering markets. It helps to get over the restrictions placed on direct and indirect entry.

Forms. Industrial licensing contracts; commercial and service licensing contracts; joint investment built on specialization between the partners; investment built on sharing in adding value.

Advantages: shares the cost of a large investment; uses the partner's strengths and distribution channels; quick and cheap entry. Disadvantages: it can create a future competitor; conflict between the partners can end the cooperation.

Worked for youAsked in two past exams

"Any company thinking of invading the international markets faces several alternative options. What are they, what are the strategies of entry, and what are their most important advantages and disadvantages?"

Another past exam asked the same thing in fewer words: "Discuss: entry models, strategies of invading international markets."

See how the answer is built

The skeleton: introduction, Option I, Option II, Option III, what the model shows, advantages and disadvantages.

Introduction. Modes of entry, also called strategies of entry or degrees of international commitment, are the forms a company can use to enter a foreign market. Any company is free to choose among three options that contain 17 modes.

Option I, commercial (export). The company only sells. Trading houses (a local trader exports the product, and the producer becomes international without any plan); agents (work for a commission and do not own the product); distributors (buy the product and resell it for their own account); international exhibitions; e-commerce. Deeper: a marketing branch, an assembly plant.

Option II, contracting (no ownership). Licensing (permission to produce and market a product for a royalty); franchising (the use of a trademark, as in McDonald's); management contracts (running someone else's project for fees) and manufacturing contracts (a local factory produces on the company's behalf); turnkey projects (build the plant and hand it over when it starts producing); international subcontracting (part of an order is produced by another party to the same specification); strategic alliances (each party uses the other's strength); joint ventures (a project owned together with a local investor).

Option III, equity (ownership). A wholly owned subsidiary; merger; acquisition, friendly (by negotiation) or hostile (through the stock market).

What the model shows. The deeper the commitment, the more market information the company collects, the more control it has over the market, and the higher the investment requirements, the risk and the profit. Host and home governments push with incentives. Modes 1 to 11 are indirect investment and 12 to 17 are direct investment. Direct investment is greenfield (from scratch) or brownfield (merger, acquisition). A company need not climb step by step, but risk avoiders enter gradually (Toshiba in Egypt: an agent first, then a joint assembly plant).

Advantages and disadvantages. The light modes need little money and carry little risk, but give little control and a lower return, and may create a future competitor. Ownership gives full control and all the profit, at high cost and high risk (nationalization, confiscation). Then give two or three points for each main mode, from lesson 11.

YoursAsked in one past exam

"What are the types of licensing contracts, and when is each type used?"

I tried it. Open the model answer

Definition. A licensing contract is a permission from one company (the licensor) to another (the licensee) to produce and market a certain product in a certain market, in return for a fee (royalty). It is an indirect mode of entry, common in industrial projects.

Licensing by necessity (forced). Used when the reasons come from the host country: (1) a small market, not profitable in the long run; (2) political and economic instability; (3) foreigners are not allowed into strategic industries; (4) restrictions on foreign ownership, full or partial; (5) restrictions on exporting and importing.

Licensing by choice. Used when the reasons come from the company: (1) no money for direct investment; (2) weak marketing ability and experience in that market; (3) high ability in research and innovation; (4) not enough people to work abroad; (5) no wish or ability to exploit the market itself; (6) selling licences instead of exporting; (7) testing the market before direct investment; (8) entering with no investment cost and the least risk.

A third case: mixing licences with industrial assembly operations.

Case

A case describes a company and asks what it should do. Answer in three moves: restate the clues in one line, name the choice, then give the reasons, each one tied to a clue.

Worked for youAsked in one past exam

"An Egyptian company wants to internationalize for the first time. It found four markets: A, B, C and D. It has no experience, and its management always avoids risk. Explain the suitable strategies to enter the market it will choose."

See how the answer is built

Move 1: the clues. First time abroad. Four candidate markets. No experience. Management avoids risk.

Move 2: the choice. First choose the market by screening the four: basic appeal, national business environment, market size and site potential, then a field trip. Then enter gradually, through indirect modes: exporting through trading houses, then an agent or a distributor, international exhibitions, e-commerce, or a licence.

Move 3: the reasons, each tied to a clue.

  • No experience: the company lacks experience and information, which are needed before a deep commitment (the Uppsala idea).
  • Avoids risk: indirect modes need little investment and carry little risk.
  • First time: indirect modes bring market information for the next step.

Closing line. When the market proves promising, the company can move to a joint venture or an assembly plant, and later to a wholly owned subsidiary.
